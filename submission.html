This project sets out to re-analyse the data in the article “Learning Biases Underlie ‘Universals’ in Avian Vocal Sequencing” by James and Sakata (2017). The article explored whether zebra finches learned song sequences through cultural propagation or biological predispositions. They drew some connections to music and speech in humans; suggesting that the sensorimotor processes shared across a variety of vertebrates may underlie “universals” in phonological, prosodic, and musical patterns in humans.

Zebra finches bred and raised in the lab were tutored with randomised, unbiased synthesised song stimuli made to sound like zebra finch song but with no sequence consistency. The sequences consisted of five typical zebra finch song elements (“syllables”) selected from males in other lab environments. The experimentally tutored finches’ “motifs”, zebra finch songs consisting of syllables arranged into stereotyped sequences, were analysed and compared to the motifs from zebra finch populations raised in labs and tutored with species-typical song stimuli. Three variables were compared when looking at the beginning, middle and end syllables: duration, frequency and amplitude.

They found that, despite being tutored with randomised songs, the zebra finches still preferred placing syllables in particular positions in the motif; preferences that mirrored ones by zebra finches tutored with species-typical song. Table 1, taken from James & Sakata (2017), shows the mean duration, amplitude and mean frequency of the beginning, middle and end syllables in both populations of finches. Both populations show a preference for longer syllables at the beginning and end of the motif than syllables in the middle, with syllables produced at the end being longer ones produced in the beginning. This re-analysis will focus on the duration of the beginning, middle and end syllables in both populations.
